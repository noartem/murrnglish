## The tag repeats, in miniature
A **question tag** is a short question stuck on the end of a statement. It repeats two things from the statement: the **auxiliary** and the **subject**.

::: rule 🧩 the recipe
1. Find the **auxiliary** in the statement (do, is, will, have, a modal…).
2. Put that same auxiliary in the tag.
3. Give the tag the **subject of the opposite person**.

_Kate won't be late_ → _Kate won't be late, **will she**?_
_You're tired_ → _You're tired, **aren't you**?_
_They won't mind_ → _They won't mind, **will they**?_
:::

::: form
# 🪞 statement → tag
You travel a lot | ==don't you?==
Sarah doesn't know Ann | ==does she?==
It didn't take long | ==did it?==
You can speak German | ==can't you?==
There are a lot of people here | ==aren't there?==
:::

**am I** is the one to watch: _I'm too impatient, **aren't I**?_ — the friendly version of _am I not?_ Many speakers prefer _right?_ or a rising intonation.

## Two jobs, one shape
::: cards
# 🤝 checking what you both know
"Great coffee, isn't it?"
"This film is long, isn't it?"
"You've been to Kyoto, haven't you?"
# 🙏 asking for something politely
"You don't have a spare key, do you?"
"Jane isn't in today, is she?"
:::

Both use the same tag. The difference is what you do with the answer — and, with a tag, the answer to a **negative** question means the opposite of the words.

::: note ⚠️ yes and no flip
_“Don't you want to go?”_
**Yes** = yes, I want to go.
**No** = no, I don't want to go.

Nobody is confused, because the tag makes clear that you expect the opposite.
:::

## Special tags
A few verbs and phrases take a fixed tag rather than a mirrored one.

::: form
# 🧷 fixed pairs
Let's go for coffee | ==shall we?==
Don't forget to call me | ==will you?==
I'd never met her before | ==had I?==
He hadn't met her before | ==had he?==
I'm not tired | ==are you?==
I think so | ==don't you think so?==
:::

::: trap
✗ ~~Kate won't be late, isn't she?~~
✓ Kate won't be late, ==will she?==
✗ ~~Sarah doesn't know Ann, don't she?~~
✓ Sarah doesn't know Ann, ==does she?==
✗ ~~You can speak German, can you?~~
✓ You can speak German, ==can't you?==
✗ ~~There are a lot of people, isn't it?~~
✓ There are a lot of people here, ==aren't there?==
The tag keeps the statement's auxiliary and its **negative** or **positive** form. Only the subject changes.
:::

::: note 🪞
_This isn't very interesting_ has the auxiliary _is_, and _is_ is already negative — so the tag asks for agreement: _This isn't very interesting, **is it**?_ Not _isn't it_.
:::

## Asking for a favour
To ask for something without sounding demanding, say the **negative** statement first and let the tag do the asking.

::: examples
- Sam, you don't have my umbrella I could use, ==do you==?
- Rosa, you couldn't give me a ride into town, ==could you==?
- Nadia, you haven't met my brother, ==have you==?
:::

The pattern is **you + negative verb … , + positive tag?** — polite precisely because you give the listener a way to say no.

::: compare
# 🎣 sounds like a demand
"Give me your pen."
"Send me the file."
# 🙏 sounds like a request
"you don't have my pen, do you?"
"you couldn't send me the file, could you?"
:::

::: note 🗣️ intonation
A tag rises at the end and sounds friendly. Fall instead and _You don't have a pen, do you?_ suddenly sounds like an accusation — a well-known trap in English.
:::

::: summary
- A tag repeats the **auxiliary** and gives the **other person's subject**.
- Two uses: **checking what you both think**, and **asking politely for something**.
- Answering a negative tag flips: **Yes** = the positive meaning.
- Fixed tags: **shall we?**, **will you?**, **am / are I?**, **had he?**
- Request pattern: **you + negative verb … , + positive tag?**
- Keep the tag rising in intonation — a falling note sounds accusing.
:::
