## How somebody looks, sounds or feels

**like** and **as if** both say: this is my impression, not a fact. I am judging by what I can see or hear.

::: compare
# 👁 like — the everyday word
- That gate ==looks like== it needs painting.
- She ==sounds like== she has a cold.
- The soup ==tastes like== salt.
# 🤔 as if — a guess you half believe
- That gate ==looks as if== it needs painting.
- She ==sounds as if== she has a cold.
- The soup ==tastes as if== it were made of salt.
:::

**as though** stands in for either one: _It sounds as though the lift is stuck._ Once you have started with **as though**, stay with it.

## It looks like… / It sounds like…

Here you report a whole scene rather than one person: your eyes for the first, your ears for the second.

::: form
# 👁 it looks like
It looks like rain. · It looks like a fire. · It looks like they've run out of milk.
# 👂 it sounds like
It sounds like an argument. · It sounds like a machine. · It sounds like bad news.
:::

In the past: _From the colour of the clouds we decided to leave early._

::: trap
✗ It looks like ==is going to rain==.
✓ It looks like ==it's going to rain==.
✗ It sounds like ==they have an argument==.
✓ It sounds like ==they're having an argument==.
✗ It looks as if ==to rain==.
✓ It looks as if ==it's going to rain==.
:::

## Any verb of doing can carry it

Not only look, sound and feel. The verb keeps the action; what follows reports the impression.

::: examples
- He ran ==like== the whole town was chasing him.
- She waved at me ==as though== we were old friends.
- He drank his coffee ==as if== he had all the time in the world.
- The gate creaked ==as if== nobody had opened it for years.
:::

After a verb of moving, **as if** often drifts towards **like**: _He ran as if the road were on fire._

## The past after as if

To talk about **now** while saying the situation is not real, **as if** takes the past. **like** does not.

::: cards
# 🎭 not true
He talks ==as if he knew== all there is to know. ==(he knows very little)==
# 😤 untrue, and it annoys you
- She is always handing me her keys, as if I were her personal assistant.
# 🤷 untrue, and nobody's fault
- People talk ==as if he were an old man==. He is thirty-one. (==as if he was==)
:::

The same past turns up after **if** and **wish**: _If I were you, I'd apologise._

## Quiz

::: quiz
? Her voice is hoarse. She ___ she has a bad cold.
= sounds as if / sounds like
? There are wet patches on the ceiling. It ___ a leak.
= looks like
? He drank his tea in five seconds. He ___ he hadn't tasted anything for a week.
= drank as if
? She apologised ___ she had ruined the evening.
= as if / as though
? Why does everyone treat him ___ he were the king?
= as if / as though
? The wind is getting up. It ___ a storm on the way.
= looks like / sounds like
? He spoke as if ___ he were reporting from the scene.
= as if / as though
? The whole room ___ it was midnight.
= feels like / feels as if
? Stop pushing the door — it ___ never been locked.
= sounds like / sounds as if
? That word — I know its shape, but it ___ English.
= looks like / looks as if
:::

::: summary
- **like**, **as if** and **as though** report how somebody looks, sounds or feels. What you say is an impression, not a fact.
- **like** is the everyday word; **as if** carries more weight, **as though** sounds a touch more formal.
- With **It looks like…** you use your eyes; with **It sounds like…** your ears.
- Almost any verb of doing can go before it: _He ran as if he were being chased._
- After **as if**, a past verb talks about a present situation that isn't real: _He talks as if he knew everything._
:::
